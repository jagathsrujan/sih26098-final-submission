Conventional 155 mm artillery shells fly unguided ballistic trajectories, and their dispersion grows with range: the published record for the fielded class runs from roughly 94 m CEP at intermediate ranges to 267 m at maximum range, against the 30 m the requirement demands at full charge and long range. Neutralising a single point target therefore demands volume fire — many rounds, long engagements, and avoidable collateral damage — and every adjustment cycle exposes the firing battery. Yantra India Limited already manufactures the mechanical hardware of these shells. What is missing is a kit that converts an existing round into a precision munition without altering the shell, the charge system or the gun: the kit occupies the fuze well and forward ogive only, so the shell itself needs no re-qualification.

We propose STHIRA-PGK: a fuze-well-mounted precision guidance kit built around a de-spun canard ring. Four fixed-incidence canards are carried on a ring that is isolated from the spinning projectile body by two bearings and is free to rotate about the roll axis. The canard arrangement is the basis of the control concept. Two canards at 0°/180° carrying equal cant angles produce codirectional aerodynamic forces: their roll torques cancel, leaving a net guidance force equal to twice one canard's force. Two canards at 90°/-90° carrying opposite cant angles produce opposing forces: zero net force, but a net roll torque about the body axis. Rotating the ring therefore aims the guidance-force vector anywhere in the plane normal to the shell axis — full two-dimensional steering from a single actuation axis, with no moving control surfaces.

The actuation is the key economy of the design. The ring carries the permanent magnets of an alternator and the body carries the stator coils, so the same machine that generates electrical power also produces control torque whenever its coils are loaded. There are no servo actuators, gearboxes or control-surface linkages, and no flight battery: the alternator supplies the kit in flight, a supercapacitor buffer carries the actuator's motoring bursts, and a reserve battery covers pre-launch and the safe state only. This directly attacks the Size, Weight, Power and Cost constraint that governs the problem statement, and it removes the least reliable components of a conventional guided shell.

Guidance is by impact-point prediction: the flight computer propagates the current state to predicted impact, computes the miss against the loaded target coordinates, and commands a ring angle that nulls it, recomputed continuously, using a GNSS/INS solution, a barometric altitude channel and an onboard aerodynamic model. The de-spun ring also solves the hardest sensing problem in a spin-stabilised guided shell: with spin removed it is the natural mounting platform for the inertial sensor, so the navigation system sees the projectile's flight dynamics rather than a 10,000–20,000 rpm rotation beyond the range of flight-representative MEMS rate sensors. The predictor carries its own error estimator: it learns in flight the biases no pre-fire data can know — the muzzle-velocity error actually realised at launch, drag bias against the model, and wind along the path already flown — and corrects for them continuously.

The nose assembly carries a multi-mode electronic fuze — proximity, time and impact — sharing the nose with the guidance electronics, with an out-of-line safe-and-arm unit interlocked on setback and spin so it cannot arm in storage, handling, bore or ramming. All modes, the time-of-flight setting and the target data are written before firing over the gun line's existing inductive fuze setter, so the kit integrates with current firing procedure. The kit also carries the predicted-miss discipline of the fielded class: if its own prediction says the round will arrive more than 150 m from the loaded target, the fuze withholds function rather than detonating where it was not aimed.

The accuracy claim is derived, not asserted. The one-sigma error budget closes at a 20.3 m root-sum-square — navigation 5 m, guidance and control 17 m, meteorological and muzzle-velocity residual 5 m, aerodynamic and model uncertainty 8 m, fuze and detonation 3 m — implying a CEP of approximately 23.9 m against the 30 m requirement. Guidance authority is not the limit: the canard pair holds at least 18.1 N across the guided window, worth 450 m of impact correction against the 407 m required to capture 80% of the unguided population — a margin of 1.11:1, stated as a margin rather than as comfort; the force floor and the correction span are derived figures, and the guided-window analysis in the verification campaign re-quantifies them. Verification is planned in three layers: a seven-degree-of-freedom trajectory simulation with the guidance loop in the loop, whose Monte Carlo dispersion analysis produces the CEP rather than asserting it; a hardware-in-the-loop bench with a rotating dynamometer rig that emulates aerodynamic torque and body spin; and a proof-of-concept assembly demonstrating ring-angle tracking under load.

Two numbers are stated plainly. Mass is the trade that is still open: the copper-and-aluminium analysis lands the kit at approximately 2.7 kg, a net penalty of about 1.7 kg over the fuze it replaces — 3.9% of the 45 kg all-up round — against a 1.4 kg target class that is not yet met; the reduction levers are the material choice, the ring diameter and retention hardware, and the gate the mass must close against is the stated requirement to retain better than 90% of the conventional round's maximum range. Cost: the target is ₹6.5 lakh (approximately US$7,800) recurring per kit at 5,000 kits per year — roughly 40% below the fielded competitor's published US$13,541 — with development and tooling excluded, so the comparison is like for like; the priced cost roll-up follows selection.

The architecture is deliberately the fielded one: the de-spun canard ring with an alternator, no actuators and one moving assembly is the published specification of the M1156 Precision Guidance Kit — the system this problem statement asks India to indigenise; the two-pair arrangement with the alternator as de-spin actuator is also claimed in a 2006 US patent, now expired. What is ours is the engineering that makes it close for this platform: the loaded-generator sizing, stated with its cost (2.92 kW peak and approximately 121 kJ per flight, leaving through a declared thermal interface rather than a hidden loss, with the dynamometer rig to confirm the figures); the ring used as an inertial platform for operation when GNSS is contested; the spin-axis GNSS aperture; the in-flight error estimator; and a state-dependent authority gate that turns the apogee authority collapse into a designed constraint. Growth is bounded and explicit: the kit carries a reserved module interface — its mechanical and electrical definition is post-selection work — for a cooperative target beacon or a fielded-type laser seeker, described as options, never as delivered capability.
